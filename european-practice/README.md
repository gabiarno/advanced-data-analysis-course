# European practice

The cohort asked to learn how this work is done in Europe. This folder is that content.

It is instructor reference material. The participant-facing version is [participant/EUROPEAN_PRACTICE.md](../participant/EUROPEAN_PRACTICE.md), which is bound into the printed handbook.

| File | What it is |
|---|---|
| [REGULATORY_FRAME.md](REGULATORY_FRAME.md) | GDPR, the Law Enforcement Directive, the AI Act and Convention 108+; which applies when; the principles that constrain a project; European public-sector data practice beyond the law |
| [CASE_STUDIES.md](CASE_STUDIES.md) | SyRI, the Dutch childcare benefits scandal, the boundary the AI Act drew afterwards, and what ordinary good practice looks like |
| [DAILY_INTEGRATION.md](DAILY_INTEGRATION.md) | Where each element attaches to each of the five days, with the minutes and the time trade that pays for it |

## How this content is meant to work

**It is methodology, not a compliance module.** Every element attaches to an analytical activity that already exists in the course. There is no standalone legal lecture and there should never be one.

**It costs no extra time.** The cohort is comfortable with Python, so the syntax teaching, guided first model and pipeline mechanics come out. That reclaimed time pays for this almost exactly — about 150 minutes across the week. `DAILY_INTEGRATION.md` shows the trade day by day.

**It leads with Europe's failures.** The two anchor cases are both European systems that harmed citizens badly enough to reach a court and bring down a government. A framework presented through its own failures is credible. A framework presented as a model to copy is a lecture, and this cohort asked how it is done, not to be told what to do.

## The line this material draws

The course is built on analytics for public administration: service demand, resource planning, emergency response, road safety, records quality, inspection scheduling and administrative processing. Every lab sits on the **resource allocation** side of the European distinction, not the **scoring people** side.

That is deliberate, and it is content rather than avoidance. For several person-scoring applications the European position is that they are prohibited or tightly restricted, so describing that position *is* the answer to how it is done here. Participants meet those applications as a governed discussion with real case law, not as a lab exercise.

When a participant raises a person-scoring problem from their own work — and someone will — reframe it with them rather than refusing it. Most have a resource-shaped sibling: which locations, which times, which process steps, which document types. That conversation is one of the most useful things they can take home.

## Before you teach it

1. **Verify currency.** Regulation moves, and a directive applies through each member state's transposing law. Check the official sources listed at the end of `REGULATORY_FRAME.md`, not summaries — including that one.
2. **Say the three limits once, on Day 1.** You are not a lawyer; this is a framework rather than legal advice; and it does not describe the participants' own national law, which is what actually governs their work.
3. **Rehearse the two case studies out loud.** They carry the whole thread, and they need to be told, not read.
4. **Brief the interpreter.** The vocabulary here — proportionality, purpose limitation, credible interval, fundamental rights, redress — is harder to interpret consistently than the statistical terms. Add it to the glossary session.
