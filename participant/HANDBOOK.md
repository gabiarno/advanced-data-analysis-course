### Optional coding pairs

If you choose the coding extension, work with one partner using two roles:

- **The analyst** watches the screen, checks the result is what was expected, and writes the finding down.

**Swap roles every 10–15 minutes.** Both roles matter equally and both are assessed. If you do not write code today, the analyst role is real work, not a consolation — the analyst writes the briefing that the group presents.

### When you are stuck

Ask for help after five minutes, not after twenty. Five minutes of being stuck is learning; twenty is waste. Use the three signals:

1. **Ready** — we have finished and have a result.
2. **Question** — we are working but want to check something.
3. **Blocked** — we cannot continue.

It also means **no result in this course is evidence about any real market**. Part of what you are practising this week is saying that out loud. That habit is the most transferable thing you will take away.

## The briefing: the format you will use all week

Every lab ends with you reporting a finding. Use this structure every time. It takes about four minutes to write and it is the format most stakeholders actually want.

> **Finding.** One sentence. The thing you found, in plain language, with the number in it.
>
> **Evidence.** One or two sentences. What you computed, on what data, compared against what.
>
> **Limitation.** One sentence. The main reason this could be wrong or could not generalise.
>
> **Next step.** One sentence. The specific, concrete thing that would reduce the uncertainty.
>
> **What would change my mind.** One sentence. What result would make you abandon this conclusion?

The last line is uncomfortable the first time. It is also the line that makes a technical person credible in a room of decision-makers, because it proves you are not defending a position.

### A worked example

> **Finding.** Store orders account for EUR 6,310 of recorded revenue against EUR 3,700 online, but this compares 60 priced Store orders with 54 priced Online orders (61 and 56 valid orders respectively), not like with like.
>
> **Evidence.** Sum of units × unit price over 116 priced orders in the cleaned synthetic table, split by channel; three valid orders have unknown prices and are excluded.
>
> **Limitation.** The excluded orders could carry any value, and a channel difference in total revenue says nothing about why — it may reflect order count, order size, or how orders were assigned to channels.
>
> **Next step.** Recover the three missing prices and compare average revenue per order rather than totals.
>
> **What would change my mind.** If average revenue per order is similar across channels, the difference is a volume story and not a channel-performance story.

### The checklist before you present

- [ ] Does my finding contain a number, and is that number correct?
- [ ] Have I said what I compared against? (A number with nothing to compare it to is not a finding.)
- [ ] Have I stated one real limitation, not a token one?
- [ ] Is my next step something a person could actually do on Monday?
- [ ] Have I avoided the words *proves*, *causes* and *optimal*?

## Choosing a chart

A chart is an argument. Pick the form that makes your argument legible, then remove everything that does not serve it.

| Your question | Chart to use | Chart to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| How does one quantity compare across a few categories? | Horizontal bar, sorted by value | Pie chart, 3D anything |
| How has one quantity changed over time? | Line chart, time on the horizontal axis | Bar chart with many bars |
| How are values distributed? | Histogram, or a box plot to compare groups | A single mean with no spread |
| Do two quantities move together? | Scatter plot | Two lines on two different vertical axes |
| How does a total break into parts? | Stacked bar, or a small sorted bar chart | Pie chart with more than four slices |
| What is one headline number? | The number itself, large, with its qualifier | A gauge, a dial, a speedometer |

### Six rules that fix most charts

1. **Label both axes, with units.** "EUR" and "minutes" are not obvious to your reader.
2. **Give it a title that states the finding**, not the contents. "Store revenue exceeds online by EUR 2,610" beats "Revenue by channel".
3. **Sort categorical bars by value**, not alphabetically, unless the order is meaningful (like months).
4. **Start bar charts at zero.** A truncated bar axis exaggerates the difference. Line charts may be truncated, but say so.
5. **Say what the data is.** Every chart in this course carries "synthetic data" and, where relevant, "priced orders only". Your charts at work should carry the equivalent.
6. **Remove the decoration.** Gridlines you do not read, borders, shadows, background fills and a legend for a single series all cost attention and return nothing.

### Three charts you will be asked to repair on Day 1

Keep these in mind when you see them — the problem in each is fixable in one change.

1. A pie chart with nine slices, four of them below 3%.
2. A bar chart of revenue whose vertical axis starts at 5,000.
3. A line chart of daily demand with no axis labels and a title that reads "Chart 1".

# Day 1 — Exploratory data analysis

## Before you start

**The question:** can I trust this table, and what can I honestly say about it?

**By the end of today you can:** audit a table for quality problems; make and document cleaning decisions; describe a distribution with the right statistic; tell an error from a genuine unusual value; and present a qualified finding.

## Key ideas

**Exploratory data analysis** is what you do before modelling: understanding what each row represents, what is recorded, what is missing, and what is wrong. Skipping it does not save time; it moves the cost to later, where it is higher.

**One row, one observation.** Before anything else, say out loud what a single row of your table represents. In today's table, one row is one order — not one customer and not one product.

**Missing is not zero.** A missing price means the price was not recorded. It does not mean the product was free. Replacing missing values with zero invents data and biases every total that follows.

**Duplicate is not always error.** Two identical rows may be a genuine repeat event or an export fault. Which one it is depends on the business rule, not on the data. In this exercise, the rule says duplicates are export errors.

**Mean and median answer different questions.** For the values 10, 12, 13, 15, 100 the mean is 30 and the median is 13. Neither is wrong. The mean answers "what would each get if we shared equally"; the median answers "what is typical". A large value moves the mean and barely moves the median.

**Spread matters as much as centre.** Standard deviation describes spread around the mean. The interquartile range describes the middle half and is less disturbed by extremes.

**An outlier is a flag, not a verdict.** It says *investigate this*. Today's 80-unit order is a legitimate bulk order and stays in the report. Today's negative quantity is invalid under the stated business rule and is excluded. The difference came from the rule, not from the number.

**Correlation is not cause.** Revenue equals units multiplied by price, so revenue and units are associated by construction. That tells you nothing about what would happen if you changed one.

# Day 2 — Machine learning for decisions

## Before you start

**The question:** can I predict something useful, and how would I know if the prediction is any good?

**By the end of today you can:** tell regression, classification and clustering apart; split data correctly before modelling; compare a model against a baseline; read a confusion matrix; explain overfitting and leakage; and say which features the model actually used.

## Key ideas

**Start with the decision, not the algorithm.** "We want to estimate travel duration before dispatch." Then ask: what information genuinely exists at that moment? Planned distance and planned stops exist. The actual arrival time does not.

**Features and target.** Features (X) are what you know when you predict. The target (y) is what you want to predict. If a feature would not be available at prediction time, it is not a feature.

**Three kinds of task.** Regression predicts a number. Classification predicts a category. Clustering groups similar observations with no target at all. The same operational situation can support all three, depending on the question.

**Split before you do anything else.** Hold out a test set and do not look at it. Use the remaining data for training and for validation. Cross-validation repeatedly splits only the training portion.

**Preprocessing goes inside the pipeline.** If you fill missing values using the whole dataset before splitting, information from the held-out data has leaked into training. Your evaluation is then optimistic and you will not find out until production.

**A baseline is not optional.** "Always predict the training mean" is a real model. If your model does not beat it, you have not shown anything. Today's regression scores about 8.43 minutes MAE against a baseline of 24.34.

**MAE is an average, not a promise.** Errors of 5, 10 and 15 minutes give an MAE of 10 minutes. It does not mean every prediction is within 10 minutes.

**Read the confusion matrix as four costs.** Correctly identified, false alarm, missed event, correctly caught. If a missed delay costs more than a false alarm, accuracy is the wrong thing to look at — look at recall and at the counts.

**Trees, forests and depth.** A tree makes successive decisions on feature values. More depth fits more detail, including noise. A forest averages many randomised trees, which usually helps stability. Neither is guaranteed to win.

**Hyperparameters are chosen on validation data.** Grid search compares candidates on training folds. Its best validation score is a *selection* statistic and tends to be optimistic. The test set stays sealed.

**Clustering does not discover truth.** Choosing k=3 produces three groups. It does not prove three groups exist. Scale your features first, because distance depends on units.

# Day 3 — Time series and natural language

## Before you start

**The question:** how do I forecast something, and how do I handle data that is not in a table at all?

**By the end of today you can:** respect time order in evaluation; identify trend and seasonality; compare a forecast against a seasonal baseline; explain ARIMA's three orders; run a text-classification pipeline; and explain how text and time connect.

## Key ideas

**Time order changes everything about evaluation.** A random split lets the model learn from the future. Always hold out the *last* period, never a random sample.

**Check the series before you model it.** Is it regularly spaced? Are there gaps? Did the definition of the measure change halfway through? A missing date usually does not mean zero.

**Trend, seasonality, cycle.** Trend is a sustained direction. Seasonality repeats at a known calendar interval — the series today has a seven-day pattern. A cycle repeats without a fixed period. Decomposition splits observed values into trend plus seasonal plus residual. The residual is what this decomposition did not explain, not proof of randomness.

**Baselines for series are strong.** "Repeat the last value" and "repeat the last seven values" are cheap and frequently beat sophisticated models. Today the weekly baseline scores about 5.53 MAE against 22.11 for a fixed non-seasonal ARIMA. That is the lesson, not an accident.

**ARIMA's three orders.** *p* uses previous observations, *d* differences the series to reduce certain non-stationary patterns, *q* models dependence on previous errors. ARIMA(1,1,1) does not represent weekly seasonality; a seasonal model needs additional specification.

**Forecast intervals are model-based.** The shaded band follows from the model's assumptions. It is not a guarantee about how often reality will fall inside it.

**One test window is weak evidence.** Real evaluation uses rolling origins: forecast from many successive cut points and look at the distribution of errors, not one number.

**Text becomes features.** A text pipeline turns documents into numbers — counting words, weighting them — then applies an ordinary classifier. Fit the vocabulary on the training set only; fitting it on everything is leakage.

**A perfect score on few examples is not a result.** Today's text model may score 100% on eight held-out English messages. That is eight messages, in one language, written by one author.

**Text and time connect.** Messages arrive with timestamps. Message volume is a time series. Category share moves over time. A text-derived signal can become a feature in a forecast — provided it was available before the moment you are forecasting.

# Day 4 — Bayesian analysis and generative models

## Before you start

**The question:** how do I reason when I am uncertain, and how do I turn uncertainty into a decision?

**By the end of today you can:** update a probability using prior and data; interpret a credible interval correctly; distinguish parameter uncertainty from outcome variability; explain what MCMC does; turn a posterior into a recommendation; and critique synthetic data.

## Key ideas

**The Bayesian question is different.** Instead of "what is the value?", it asks "what is the distribution of plausible values, given what I believed before and what I have now seen?"

**Prior, likelihood, posterior.** The prior is what you believed before this data. The likelihood says how compatible the data is with each possible value. The posterior is the updated belief: posterior is proportional to likelihood times prior.

**Today's exact example.** Prior Beta(2,18), mean 0.10. Observe 8 defective out of 100. Posterior is Beta(10,110), mean about 0.0833. The posterior mean is not 0.08 because the prior still contributes. More data reduces the prior's relative influence.

**A credible interval is not a confidence interval.** A 95% credible interval contains 95% of the posterior probability *under this model and this prior*. That is a statement about belief given assumptions, not a guarantee.

**Parameter uncertainty is not outcome variability.** You may be very confident the defect rate is near 8%, and still be unable to predict how many defects appear in the next batch of 50. These are two different questions and they need two different calculations.

**MCMC in three actions.** Propose a nearby value. Compare its posterior density to the current one. Accept it, or stay. Staying is part of the chain, not a missing measurement. Discard an initial warm-up. Run several chains from different starts and check they agree.

**High acceptance is not good on its own.** Tiny proposals get accepted constantly while the chain barely moves. Look at the trace and the autocorrelation, not only the acceptance rate.

**From posterior to decision.** The useful output is rarely the estimate. It is a statement like: "there is a 12% posterior probability that the defect rate exceeds the 10% contractual limit." That number triggers an action; the point estimate does not.

**Synthetic data inherits its source.** Generated data reproduces the patterns of what generated it, including its biases and its errors. Matching a mean is easy; matching a distribution, the tails and the relationships between variables is not. Synthetic data is not automatically anonymous, and generating it does not by itself discharge a privacy obligation.

# Day 5 — Big data, advanced models and capstone

## Before you start

**The question:** what changes when the data does not fit on one machine, and what would it take to put any of this into production?

**By the end of today you can:** explain partitioned aggregation; run Spark DataFrame and SQL operations; evaluate a Spark MLlib model against a baseline; distinguish distributed computing from deep learning; explain generator and discriminator roles; and state what production readiness requires.

## Key ideas

**"Big data" is an engineering constraint, not a row count.** What matters is volume, arrival rate, format, the computation required, and available memory. Small workloads are often faster in pandas. Distribution buys capacity and costs coordination.

**Partitioned aggregation.** Compute partial results on each partition, then combine matching keys. Partition 1 gives A:15, B:20; partition 2 gives C:15, B:3; combined gives A:15, B:23, C:15. That idea is the whole of MapReduce.

**The Hadoop ecosystem has distinct parts.** HDFS is distributed storage. YARN manages resources. MapReduce is a processing framework. Spark is a *separate* execution engine that can integrate with some of those parts. Keep them distinct — conflating them is the most common error on this topic.

**Spark is lazy.** Transformations such as `filter` and `groupBy` build a plan. Actions such as `count`, `show` and `collect` trigger execution. Grouping usually requires a shuffle, which moves data between partitions and is where the time goes.

**`collect()` is dangerous by default.** It pulls every result row into the driver's memory. Safe for a two-row aggregate; unsafe as a habit.

**Spark on your laptop is still your laptop.** Running two local threads demonstrates the API works. It demonstrates nothing about cluster performance.

**Deep learning and distributed computing are separate ideas.** A neural network is a model family. Distribution is how you run computation. You can have either without the other.

**GANs: two models competing.** A generator produces candidate data; a discriminator tries to tell generated from real. Each improves against the other. A toy scalar GAN in a classroom illustrates the mechanism and proves nothing about generating realistic data.

**Production readiness is not "the code runs".** It needs: representative data with clear governance; agreed error costs; a monitored performance metric; a defined retraining trigger; a plan for what happens when the model is wrong; and a named owner. A model nobody monitors is a model quietly degrading.
